## Blockers

1. **`sloth_slow_life` c5 (`partial`, both locales), plus the summary, cardText, card block 2 and quiz 3's explanation:** the lesson says as a plain fact that the algae in the fur camouflage the sloth ("algae in their fur camouflage them"; "algas no pelo que a camuflam"). Britannica backs only the greenish tinge. The lesson's own scholarly sources treat camouflage as unproven. BMC Evolutionary Biology says the algae are "widely believed to … provide the sloth with effective camouflage. However, this phenomenon has not been explored in any detail and there is little evidence to substantiate this widely held opinion". Proc. R. Soc. B says "it is possible that algal cultivation enhances sloth survival via camouflage" and speculates that camouflage is "secondary". This dispute is unmarked, and it sits in the lesson's headline text. Hedge it everywhere it appears, for example "are thought to help camouflage" / "que, acredita-se, ajudam a camuflá-la". Quiz 3's correct answer ("algae growing in the fur") is right. Only its explanation needs the hedge.
2. **`immortal_jellyfish` c6 (high-stakes, `partial`, both locales; card block 2 and quiz 2):** "In nature, most probably die from predators or disease before they can revert" rests on one publisher, Wikipedia. The second support (BBC) is the sentence "scientists can't rule out that it doesn't eventually die naturally". That is about natural death, not predation or disease, so it does not back the claim. A high-stakes claim needs two publishers. The BBC snapshot already has usable text: "in the wild, there's plenty of things that probably do kill them – including fluctuating availability of their plankton food, predators or algae that smother jellyfish colonies". Cite that excerpt for c6. "Disease" and "most" would then still come from Wikipedia alone. Either soften to "predators and other hazards" or find a second source for disease. Move the "can't rule out natural death" excerpt to a claim of its own: the deep dive's last sentence has no claim of its own today.

## Editorial notes

1. **`gecko_feet` c13 (`partial`):** the year 2002 for the PNAS study (Autumn et al.) does not appear in the snapshot. Its reference list stops at 2001, which fits, but no excerpt states the year. The rest of the claim is backed: in 2000 the Berkeley release could not rule out water, and PNAS found equal adhesion on surfaces that repel water and surfaces that attract it. Add a dated excerpt or record `publishedAt`.
2. **`gecko_feet` card block 2, takeaway and c5:** "billions of spatulae" / "bilhões de espátulas". The Berkeley release says "a billion pads" ("these billion spatulae"). "About a billion" would match the source.
3. **`gecko_feet` deep dive, last paragraph:** "The idea has inspired dry adhesive tapes … Not every surface works, though: geckos cannot stick to Teflon." The cited tape paper (PNAS, carbon-nanotube gecko tape) says the tape "sticks to a variety of surfaces, including Teflon". As written, readers may think the tape fails on Teflon too. The claim itself (c16, about geckos) is right. Consider "The gecko itself cannot stick to Teflon, though the nanotube tape can."
4. **`octopus_colour` c13 (`partial`) and quiz 3's prompt:** the year 2024 for the PNAS energetic-cost study is not in the snapshot. The snapshot's latest date is "Deposited 7 July 2023". The finding itself is backed word for word. Add a dated excerpt or record `publishedAt`. Similarly, the deep dive's "In 2015" for the skin-light (LACE) study is in no claim, and the J. Exp. Biol. snapshot does not give its year. Frontiers cites it as "Ramirez and Oakley, 2015". For c8, "2016" is in the Frontiers snapshot ("Stubbs and Stubbs (2016a)"), but not in c8's own excerpts, so I marked c8 `supported`. Adding that excerpt to c8 would make the date checkable.
5. **`octopus_colour` summary and quiz 1 prompt:** "opened and closed by nerve-controlled muscles" / "O que abre e fecha os cromatóforos". The sources say the muscles stretch the sac open when they contract ("When chromatophore muscles contract, the pigment sac at the center is stretched out"). None says the muscles close it: the sac is elastic. The card body and quiz 1's explanation have it right. "Opened by nerve-controlled muscles" would be more exact. This doesn't change what readers learn about speed, so it is not a blocker.
6. **`sloth_slow_life` c6 and c9 (`partial`):** "a 2014 study" / "a 2014 hypothesis" (Pauli et al., Proc. R. Soc. B). The year does not appear in the snapshot. The findings themselves are backed word for word. Add a dated excerpt or record `publishedAt`.
7. **`sloth_slow_life` c3 (high-stakes):** two publishers back it together, but for different halves. "The slowest rate of digestion for any mammal" comes only from Proc. R. Soc. B, and "a meal can take up to a week" only from Britannica. That is acceptable, but a second source for the superlative would be firmer.
8. **`tardigrades_space` takeaway (both locales) and card block 2:** "what hurt them most was the Sun's ultraviolet radiation". The review (OLEB) lists "ultraviolet solar radiation, ionising solar radiation and galactic cosmic radiation" together as factors that "significantly reduced" survival, without ranking them. ESA quotes Jönsson saying that vacuum "and cosmic radiation" were not a problem. The snapshots back "UV radiation cut survival", but not "most". "Sharply" also reads more into "significantly" than the source says. Consider "radiation from the Sun, especially ultraviolet, cut survival".
9. **`immortal_jellyfish` c12 (and c6):** these rest on Wikipedia, which here relays its own references. c12 is "the reversal has never been observed in the wild, partly because it is fast". These claims are fine for a draft, but citing the underlying papers, or the BBC and AMNH where they say the same, would be firmer.
